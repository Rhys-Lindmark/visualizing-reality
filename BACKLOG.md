# How Everything Evolved — Continuous Backlog

## North star

Build an Our World in Data–style guide to the rest of history: visual-first,
quantitatively honest, source-traceable, and opinionated enough to teach a real
model of how societies changed. Begin with the world before industrialization.

The recurring question is **domestication** in its widest sense: how humans
domesticated landscapes, water, plants, animals, metal, labor, information,
belief, violence, and one another—and how those systems remade humans in turn.

Use the explanatory ambition of ACOUP, Peter Turchin, and Saloni Dattani as a
quality bar. Do not impersonate them or turn any one framework into dogma.

## The page contract

Each subject becomes a real page, not a miscellaneous section. A finished page has:

1. A short introduction that states the historical problem.
2. Exactly five core insights, ordered as an argument rather than a fact list.
   Present them as five clickable tabs: one selected argument at a time, with
   editorial text on the left and its visualization on the right.
3. At least one excellent map or timeline and four other useful visualizations.
4. Downloadable data or a transparent visual model with source keys and notes.
5. A compact methodology and sources section that a skeptical historian can audit.

The visual language should feel like Our World in Data: direct claim headlines,
calm typography, generous whitespace, legible annotations, useful toggles, and
graphics that explain the claim without decorative clutter.

## P0 — Site-wide five-claim editorial audit

Re-evaluate all thirty-five insight slots as arguments, not as inherited chart topics. For every page, begin with the five questions a curious reader most wants answered: What happened? Why did this polity or system win, grow, or become distinctive? How did it work at its height? Why did it change, fragment, or fail? What consequential legacy followed? The exact questions may vary, but each selected tab and its article headline must be one concise, memorable, falsifiable historical claim backed by a visual that directly bears on that claim. Prefer claims that strong historians find important, surprising, and broadly defensible; test ACOUP, Turchin, Scott, Cremieux Recueil, Saloni Dattani, and other syntheses against primary evidence and current scholarship rather than treating any writer as authority.

Do not use historiographical cautions as the main claim: “there was no single birthday,” “archives are not diets,” “the evidence is complicated,” “different clocks,” “not one score,” and “this does not prove” belong in the note or Methods unless the disagreement itself is the historically important result. Do not lead with artifact counts merely because they are measurable. Start from the substantive explanation, then use the best available quantitative anchors, maps, documents, images, or transparent reconstruction. If no defensible visual supports a proposed headline, change the headline. Respect image licenses and permissions; use linked scholarship as a discovery and interpretive source, not as permission to copy its figures.
