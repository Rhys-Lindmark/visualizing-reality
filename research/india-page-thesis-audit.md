# India from cities to empires — five-question editorial audit

## Page problem

The page should explain how South Asia repeatedly coordinated people and resources across large distances even when political centers were plural, regional, short-lived, or archaeologically difficult to see. It should not collapse four millennia into a single civilization, religion, state tradition, or modern national geography.

## Five reader questions and candidate claims

1. **What was most distinctive about the Indus urban system?** Candidate accepted for insight 01: **Indus cities coordinated without monumentalizing rulers.** Test shared metrology directly, then pair it with the durable archaeological absence of securely identified palaces, elaborate royal tombs, large temples, and ruler-aggrandizing monuments. Do not infer that rulers, elites, coercion, inequality, or one centralized state were absent.
2. **Why did South Asian settlement and political rhythms differ so much by region?** Candidate for insight 02: **Monsoon diversity kept one political rhythm from fitting all India.** Test winter and summer cropping, rainfall, rivers, irrigation, and settlement seasonality with regionally specific archaeobotanical and paleoclimate evidence. Reject any one-number monsoon or hydraulic-state score.
3. **How and why did large cities and territorial states return in the first millennium BCE?** Candidate for insight 03: **Iron Age cities grew with coins, roads, and competing states.** Test whether urban footprints, fortified centers, punch-marked coin finds, and political chronologies move together; do not assume iron or coinage alone caused urbanization.
4. **How did Mauryan power work across an enormous and uneven realm?** Candidate for insight 04: **Mauryan rule was strongest in cores and thinner at frontiers.** Compare inscription locations and languages, provincial centers, roads, and direct administrative evidence. Do not turn Ashoka's edict distribution into a complete boundary or equal-intensity control map.
5. **What consequential systems outlasted individual states?** Candidate for insight 05: **Religions and trade traveled farther than Indian empires.** Compare dated inscriptions, monastic sites, merchant evidence, ports, and transmitted texts without treating religious affiliation or trade contact as one continuous political sphere.

## Rejected opening claims

- **“The Indus was extensive, standardized, and unreadable.”** Accurate but descriptive; it does not explain why the combination matters.
- **“The Indus was a centralized state.”** Shared weights do not identify a ruler, capital, or political hierarchy.
- **“The Indus had no rulers.”** The undeciphered script and uneven archaeological survival make this stronger claim unsupportable.
- **“The Indus was egalitarian.”** Green's synthesis makes a serious case, but the page's direct evidence can support only the narrower claim about coordination and the absence of familiar monumental ruler signals.
- **“Standard weights prove a market economy.”** The weights were often small, and published scholarship warns against treating them as bulk-goods measures or a census of everyday transactions.

## Sequencing

Open with a counterexample to the assumption that coordination requires visible kings. Move next to ecological plurality; then to urban and political reassembly; then to imperial reach; end with networks whose geography exceeded states.
