# Bronze Age chariot system — evidence audit

## Claim

Bronze Age chariots were not stand-alone wonder weapons. Their usable speed depended on a linked system of horse teams, vehicles, control gear, specialist people, upkeep, and institutions able to reproduce knowledge and matériel.

This is a qualitative dependency claim. The evidence does not support one cross-regional estimate of force size, military effectiveness, annual cost, fodder acreage, or a universal crew standard.

## Five deliberately unlike windows

| Window | Directly visible anchor | What remains unavailable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Husiatyn, fifteenth century BCE | two complete horses and antler bridle elements in an elite burial | no wooden vehicle, battle use, training intensity, or force total |
| Hattusha, fifteenth-century original / thirteenth-century copy | at least 184 reconstructed training days plus feed, water, stable, pasture, blankets, massage, and exercise instructions | ancient numbered total, compliance, force size, or outcomes |
| Pylos, final palatial horizon | 58 recorded wheel pairs, 13 unfit for service | complete chariots, horses, crews, annual output, or repair time |
| EA 15, c. 1347–1330 BCE | one chariot and two horses sent together as a greeting gift | price, standard issue, itinerary, support costs, or battlefield use |
| Anyang, late Shang | M40 has one chariot, two horses, and three people; other contexts preserve fittings and repair tools | deployed formation, army total, universal crew, or a first-in-China date |

## Source decisions

### Paired horses without a vehicle

Makarowicz and colleagues combine radiocarbon, artefact, zooarchaeological, and ancient-DNA evidence for the Husiatyn burial. The horses and antler bridle elements support a paired traction package, but no wooden vehicle survived. The paper itself allows chariot and/or cart use. The chart therefore makes the absent vehicle conspicuous.

### Training as an institution

Raulwing’s study of CTH 284 distinguishes the lost fifteenth-century original from the thirteenth-century copy and reports a reconstructed schedule of at least 184 days. The number is not a surviving ancient day count. It is shown with a ≥ sign and kept attached to the feed, water, care, and exercise instructions that make it historically meaningful.

### Wheels are not chariots

O’Brien reports the Pylian Sa-series tally from Ventris and Chadwick: 58 wheel pairs, 13 unfit for service. Both the source and this project reject converting that fragmentary inventory into a chariot-force size. Condition recording is the claim: the archive makes inspection, spares, and maintenance visible.

### One diplomatic package

ORACC’s translation of EA 15 gives one good chariot and two horses as Aššur-uballiṭ I’s greeting gift. The Metropolitan Museum’s contextual essay locates the tablet in great-king diplomacy. This is a direct bundle in one message, not a standard equipment table or army inventory.

### Mortuary package and repair knowledge

The Academia Sinica museum account describes M40 as one disassembled chariot, two horses, a few weapons, and three people. Rawson and colleagues’ open synthesis identifies specialist charioteers, control fittings, and axes, chisels, and related tools interpreted for repairing chariot woodwork in other Anyang contexts. The public row explicitly states that M40 and M41 are different pits and that mortuary staging is not field organization.

## Blocked inferences

- No additive readiness, completeness, technological-advantage, or military-effectiveness score.
- No conversion from wheels to complete or deployable chariots.
- No army total from a burial, archive, gift, or mortuary formation.
- No universal horse team, crew, or repair-tool standard.
- No hay, grain, pasture, land, labor, or annual-cost estimate without local source evidence.
- No assumption that missing evidence means a dependency was absent.
- No first-invention date from the five selected windows.

## Reproducibility

The canonical five-row dataset is `public/data/bronze-chariot-systems.csv`. The browser reads the byte-identical immutable snapshot at `public/data/bronze-age/20260830-chariot1/bronze-chariot-systems.csv`. Automated validation requires all five cases, source keys, bounded anchors, negative inference limits, and snapshot equality, and rejects fields that would introduce a force total, effectiveness score, annual cost, fodder acreage, or crew standard.
