# Iron Age insight 04 — mobilization evidence audit

## Question

When did cheaper iron equipment widen military participation, and why did that
not mechanically produce large or durable armies?

The visual treats equipment as one line in a mobilization ledger. Recruitment,
supply, command, finance, and replacement remain separate requirements. It does
not multiply soldiers by kilograms of iron, infer army size from artifact counts,
or create a military-power score.

## Accepted evidence windows

| Case | What is retained | Evidence status | What it can test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New Kingdom Egypt at Kadesh, c. 1274 BCE | Four named divisions are recorded; approximately 20,000 is Breasted's inference from the division size discussed in Papyrus Anastasi I | documented structure plus modern inference | A territorial state could organize a large field army before iron was the ordinary organizing material |
| Pre-Roman European civitates, c. 500–100 BCE | Devereaux's specialist synthesis links cheaper simple iron weapons with a shift from aristocratic retinues toward broader client levies, while also emphasizing interstate pressure and social organization | qualitative synthesis across diverse regions and centuries | Lower equipment cost could widen participation without independently creating recruitment or logistics |
| Carthage, 215 BCE | Taylor reconstructs approximately 170,000 total strategic deployment, mostly subjects, subaltern allies, and mercenaries rather than citizens | modern strategic-deployment model | A different political architecture could reach roughly Roman scale in the same war |
| Rome and Italian allies, 212 BCE | Taylor reconstructs approximately 185,000 across about twenty-five legions and allied formations | modern strategic-deployment model | Rome's unusually broad citizen–colony–allied extraction system, not iron alone, explains the peak |

Only the Carthaginian and Roman values share an author, model family, strategic
scope, and war context. They alone receive proportional bars. Kadesh is shown as
an explicitly inferred campaign total; the pre-Roman case receives no headcount.

## Source decisions

- Nicholas Wernick, *The Logistics of the New Kingdom Egyptian Military in the
  Levant* (University of Liverpool PhD, 2014), pp. 245–246. The thesis states
  that the Egyptian starting total is not recorded, that four divisions
  accompanied Ramesses II, and that the approximately 20,000 total is a modern
  inference. <https://doi.org/10.17638/00018573>
- Bret Devereaux, “How to Raise a Tribal Army in Pre-Roman Europe, Part III”
  (2024). This is used as specialist synthesis, not as a numeric dataset. It
  explicitly treats cheaper iron as one factor alongside state pressure and
  social mobilization. <https://acoup.blog/2024/06/21/collections-how-to-raise-a-tribal-army-in-pre-roman-europe-part-iii-going-to-war-with-the-army-you-have/>
- Michael J. Taylor, *Soldiers and Silver* (University of Texas Press, 2020),
  especially the Roman and rival-manpower chapters. Taylor's values are models
  of total strategic deployment, not observed battlefield counts.
  <https://doi.org/10.7560/321683>
- Michael J. Taylor, “Carthaginian Manpower,” Society for Classical Studies.
  The official abstract reports the approximately 170,000 peak in 215 BCE and
  identifies the major recruitment categories. <https://classicalstudies.org/carthaginian-manpower>

## Prohibited inferences

- no kilograms of iron per soldier or army-level iron tonnage;
- no army headcount inferred from graves, weapons, slag, furnaces, or ore;
- no conversion of Kadesh's campaign inference into a whole-state deployment;
- no numeric headcount for the pre-Roman civitas synthesis;
- no direct comparison bar between Kadesh and the Punic War strategic models;
- no claim that Rome's 15,000 modeled advantage over Carthage was precise or
  decided the war;
- no claim that iron was necessary or sufficient for mass mobilization;
- no causal share, readiness score, replacement curve, or military-power index.

## Editorial conclusion

Cheaper iron could lower the equipment threshold for a broader levy. The scale
and persistence of an army still depended on who could compel service, who paid
and provisioned the force, how commands traveled, and how losses were replaced.
Metal did not mobilize itself.
