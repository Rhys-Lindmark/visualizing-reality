# Iron Age insight 05 — production institutions audit

## Claim under test

Iron production became durable through multiple institutional architectures. A royal arsenal, a household exchange network, a distribution-centred settlement system, a state monopoly with frontier gaps, and a long-lived urban production landscape are not points on one centralization ladder.

## Inclusion rule

Each case must expose the same five questions—inputs, making, coordination, distribution, and visible evidence—while keeping its evidence class and inferential limit. Unlike observations never receive a shared output, productivity, market-integration, state-capacity, or centralization score.

## Cases retained

### Neo-Assyrian Kalhu

- Stronach's excavation report identifies Fort Shalmaneser as a royal depot and documents military metalwork in a destruction context.
- ORACC's scholarly Nimrud project describes workshops, magazines, barracks, military repair and storage, and a clay docket recording 36,242 inspected bows.
- The bow count demonstrates administrative equipment handling. It is not an iron-output or total-arsenal figure.

### Sangis and Vivungi

- Bennerhag and colleagues directly document furnaces, metallurgical debris, scientific analyses, and the separation between Sangis 842 and Sangis 730.
- Their chemical and spatial synthesis proposes several decentralized units, multiple household stakeholders, and a centrally placed smithing site.
- That organizational reconstruction is retained as a published inference rather than a named ownership system.

### La Tène Bohemia

- Salač's regional interpretation makes a useful distinction between production sites and distribution nodes, arguing that oppida should not automatically be treated as dominant manufacturing or political centres.
- The visual labels this as a regional synthesis. It does not turn “distribution” into proof of free markets, private ownership, weak politics, or a universal model of oppida.

### Han Lingnan

- Zhang and colleagues combine preliminary archaeometallurgy with the 119 BCE monopoly and the *Han Shu* statement that Lingnan lacked a *tieguan*.
- Their import interpretation is retained but not converted into a complete provenance census.
- State policy and a regional administrative gap are shown together; neither is allowed to erase local bloomery evidence or mixed technologies.

### Meroe and Hamadab

- Humphris and Scheibner's chronology uses 97 radiocarbon dates from nine slag mounds and supports a potentially thousand-year production history with distinct spatial phases.
- The authors explicitly warn that slag volume, deposition, ore, and efficiency complicate output inference.
- Proximity to the Royal City does not by itself prove a royal monopoly, palace ownership, or one management regime across the full span.

## Rejected constructions

- Tonnes of iron from slag volume.
- Annual output, labor totals, or workshop productivity.
- One “centralization” or “state capacity” score.
- A sequence from household to market to state as progressive stages.
- Treating a royal-city location as proof of royal ownership.
- Treating absence of an iron office in one text as absence of production or administration.
- Converting a bow-inspection docket into iron or army totals.
