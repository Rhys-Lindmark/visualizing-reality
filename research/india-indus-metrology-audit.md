# India insight 01 — Indus coordination and monumental power

## Accepted claim

**Indus cities coordinated without monumentalizing rulers.**

The claim joins two independently sourced observations. First, published excavation series at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro repeatedly approximate the same binary-decimal weight ratios. Second, after a century of excavation, archaeologists still lack securely identified palaces, elaborate royal tombs, large temples, and ruler-aggrandizing monuments comparable to those in several contemporary early states.

## Quantitative transcription

The chart transcribes the eight ratios shared by all three series in J. Mark Kenoyer's 2010 Table 9.3: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 160. The series are the Harappa Archaeological Research Project, M. S. Vats's Harappa excavation, and E. J. H. Mackay's Mohenjo-daro excavation. Each plotted value is:

`published mean mass in grams / published category ratio`

The reference line is 0.857 grams, Hemmy's fitted unit from the 1931 official excavation publication. The calculation is a transparent normalization, not a new estimate. All twenty-four means and their sample sizes are preserved in the downloadable CSV. The four-observation HARP ratio-160 mean yields 0.755 grams per unit and remains visible.

## What the evidence supports

- Harappa and Mohenjo-daro used recognizably shared weight ratios.
- Multiple excavation series cluster near the fitted small unit across a wide range of denominations.
- Shared conventions could coordinate transactions or administrative practices across distant cities.
- Familiar monumental signals of individualized rulers have not been securely identified.

## What it does not support

- One centralized Indus empire, king, capital, bureaucracy, or enforcement mechanism.
- The absence of rulers, elites, inequality, coercion, temples, or ritual authority.
- A measured tolerance imposed on every weight or transaction.
- Bulk-goods flows, prices, market share, trade volume, tax receipts, or everyday use prevalence.
- Equal precision across series: sample sizes range from one to ninety-four.
- A claim that the 1931 “500 miles” is a modern geodesic measurement; it is retained as the excavation report's approximate comparison.
