# Persia insight 04 evidence audit — the army was an assembly

## Published claim

Achaemenid armies were assembled from distinct military systems rather than standardized into one force. Imperial institutions connected contributing formations to senior command, campaign supply, and a battlefield plan. That interface created scale, but orders, terrain, timing, and local command choices still determined whether the assembly worked.

## Evidence windows

1. **Doriscus, 480 BCE:** Herodotus 7.61–99 names infantry, cavalry, and naval peoples, equipment, and commanders. How and Wells count 61 people-labels across 46 infantry, eight cavalry, and twelve naval categories. Because categories overlap, these are catalogue labels—not army shares or force totals.
2. **Advance depots, 480 BCE:** Herodotus 7.25 names five zones where grain was sent before the march: Leuce Acte, Tyrodiza, Doriscus, Eion, and Macedonia. The text gives no surviving quantities or capacities.
3. **Imperial fleet, 480 BCE:** Herodotus's naval catalogue preserves regional maritime contingents and equipment. His ship totals and rankings of seamanship are excluded.
4. **Cunaxa, 401 BCE:** Xenophon supplies a participant narrative of Cyrus's mixed army, the order to Clearchus, the battle, and four hundred reported provision wagons. The Encyclopaedia Iranica reconstruction rejects Xenophon's headline Persian totals and identifies the uncoordinated maneuver as decisive. Xenophon did not witness every command exchange.
5. **Gaugamela, 331 BCE:** Arrian 3.11 says Aristobulus based the Persian order of battle on a captured written scheme. The surviving second-century narrative is useful for command zones and named formations, not for its enormous force totals.
6. **Persepolis, 509–494 BCE:** the Fortification archive documents food distribution, commodity transport, state reserves, fodder, travelers, messengers, and road-station accounting in Persis and Elam. It is a regional administrative substrate, not Xerxes's campaign ledger.

## Source hierarchy

- Primary or ancient narrative anchors: Herodotus, Xenophon, and Arrian.
- Administrative counterweight: the Persepolis Fortification archive through Hallock and Dandamayev.
- Specialist checks: Encyclopaedia Iranica entries on the pre-Islamic army, Cunaxa, and Arrian.
- How and Wells is used only for its transparent editorial count of Herodotus's catalogue categories, not as current military analysis.

## Deliberate exclusions

- no acceptance, reconciliation, or replacement of ancient headline army totals;
- no ethnic share, combat-power score, equipment score, discipline rank, or national effectiveness rank;
- no conversion of catalogue labels into soldiers, formations, or battlefield presence;
- no assumption that the Doriscus catalogue is an exact order of battle;
- no claim that the Persepolis archive is an empire-wide military logistics census;
- no claim that Clearchus deliberately disobeyed rather than misunderstood, prioritized flank security, or received an imperfectly transmitted order;
- no claim that Arrian's captured-document story makes every Gaugamela detail contemporary or certain.
