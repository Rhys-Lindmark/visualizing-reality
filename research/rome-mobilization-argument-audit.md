# Rome insight 02 — why Rome won

## Reader-facing claim

**Rome won because its Italian alliance could survive defeat.**

The comparison is deliberately narrower than a general ranking of Roman military effectiveness. Michael J. Taylor's same-model reconstruction places Carthaginian strategic deployment at approximately 170,000 troops in 215 BCE and Rome with the Italian allies at approximately 185,000 in 212 BCE. Carthage could therefore match Rome's scale during the war. The more defensible difference is the political architecture behind repeated mobilization: a durable core of Roman citizens, Latin colonies, and Italian allies continued to furnish forces after catastrophic battlefield losses.

## Evidence kept separate

- Taylor's two totals are modeled simultaneous strategic deployments, not battlefield counts or censuses.
- Devereaux's 125-versus-100 worked-metal comparison is a bounded relative panoply index for heavy infantry in the third and second centuries BCE. It is not kilograms, a full-polity equipment average, or a time series.
- The longer Roman force-estimate chart mixes Republican wartime deployment with later establishments and therefore remains a secondary evidence view with visible gaps.

## Claims rejected

- Rome won because Carthage could not raise a comparably large force.
- A modeled 15,000-man gap is a precise victory margin.
- Iron abundance or cheaper Roman equipment caused mass mobilization.
- Worked metal per soldier can be multiplied by troop totals into a combat-power score.
- Sparse observations justify annual replacement curves for Rome or Carthage.
