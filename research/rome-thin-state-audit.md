# Rome insight 03 audit — governing through cities

## Decision

The primary claim is: **Rome governed through cities, not a giant bureaucracy.**

Western Han and Rome ruled populations of similar order. The transmitted Han
establishment records 130,285 civil officials in 5 BCE, excluding military
officials. Li Feng summarizes a published comparison that this was roughly
twenty times contemporary Roman officialdom. Comparative work edited by Walter
Scheidel explains the institutional difference: Han cities were controlled
through centrally appointed officials, while the High Roman Empire depended
far more on municipal structures and relatively autonomous local elites for
day-to-day administration.

The visual therefore combines one recorded count, one explicitly approximate
ratio, and a qualitative mechanism comparison. It does not reverse-engineer a
precise Roman headcount from the ratio.

## Candidate claims tested

### Military-fiscal state

The earlier visual correctly showed Duncan-Jones's reconstructed army share of
central expenditure. But the comparison is mostly intramural: no sufficiently
like-for-like cross-polity budget denominator was available. Alfani, Bolla, and
Scheidel also describe Rome's military-heavy expenditure as closely connected
to its small bureaucracy. The fiscal evidence remains registered and
downloadable, but it no longer occupies one of five primary Rome claims.

### Slavery and coerced production

Slavery was central to Roman society, especially in Italy, but empire-wide
slave totals, sectoral shares, and changes over time are too uncertain for the
simple comparative graph this slot requires. A Rome page should eventually
address slavery directly; it should not manufacture a clean denominator here.

### Urban provisioning and spectacle

The grain dole and monumental entertainment are vivid and measurable in places,
but their prominence in the capital does not make them the organizing principle
of imperial government. Beneficiary counts are also not a central-budget share
or an empire-wide welfare measure.

### City-mediated administration

This candidate has the strongest combination of surprise, comparative evidence,
and explanatory reach. It helps explain how Rome governed widely, why municipal
elites mattered, why Roman urbanism spread, and why Roman administration looked
different from Han rule at similar population scale.

## Evidence and limits

- Ban Gu, *Han shu* 7a.743, is the transmitted basis for the Han establishment.
- Hans Bielenstein, *The Bureaucracy of Han Times* (1980), reports 130,285
  officials from chancellor through accessory clerks in 5 BCE and notes the
  exclusion of military officials.
- Li Feng, *Bureaucracy and the State in Early China* (2008), reports the same
  count and the published approximate twenty-to-one Han–Rome comparison.
- Walter Scheidel, ed., *State Power in Ancient China and Rome* (2015), provides
  the comparative framework: Roman bureaucracy developed later and municipal
  structures mattered more in Roman day-to-day administration.
- Guido Alfani, Michele Bolla, and Walter Scheidel (2025) independently describe
  Rome's small bureaucracy, municipal tax collection, alliance with provincial
  elites, and Han's much larger bureaucracy.

The 20× figure is an order of magnitude. Office coverage is not perfectly
matched; Roman municipal elites are part of the governing mechanism but not the
imperial payroll; the Han count excludes military officials; and the selected
dates are near-contemporary rather than identical. The visual states each limit
instead of converting the ratio into a false Roman census.
