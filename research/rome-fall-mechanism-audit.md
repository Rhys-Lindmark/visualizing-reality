# Rome insight 04 audit — why the western court fell

## Decision

The primary claim is: **The West fell when war stripped away the taxes that
paid its armies.**

This is a feedback mechanism, not a one-cause slogan. Armed migrations and
invasions created severe external pressure. Recurrent civil wars diverted
Roman armies and political attention. Lost provinces then stopped supporting
the court that would have had to reconquer them. The 439 loss of Carthage was
the clearest fiscal shock, and the failed 461 and 468 reconquests left the
western court without its strongest route back to a larger tax base.

## Alternatives tested

### Plague and climate

The Antonine plague is too early and the Justinianic plague too late to explain
the fifth-century end of the western court. Earlier demographic and economic
weakening may have reduced resilience, but the current public evidence does not
identify a plague shock that maps onto the 395–476 western outcome closely
enough for the primary graph.

### A single barbarian military breakthrough

External coalitions mattered, but Rome had survived earlier migrations and the
eastern court survived major fifth-century attacks. A simple enemy-strength
story cannot explain why territorial losses became progressively harder for the
West to reverse. The fiscal–military loop supplies that missing mechanism.

### Internal political failure alone

Civil wars consumed capacity and repeatedly changed command, but they did not
remove the external pressure or make territorial outcomes automatic. The claim
therefore joins internal conflict to invasion and revenue loss rather than
assigning a causal percentage to elite competition.

### Christianity, decadence, or one symbolic sack

These do not provide a measurable mechanism that explains the divergent fate
of the eastern and western courts. The 410 sack remains historically important
but is not treated as the moment the empire ended.

## Quantitative anchors

- Valentinian III's Novel XIII (445) withdrew seven parts of the former
  assessment for Numidia and Mauretania Sitifensis. This is one-eighth remaining
  in two named damaged provinces, not one-eighth of all western revenue.
- Peter Heather converts the lost or damaged African revenues into annual
  maintenance equivalents using Hugh Elton's cost assumptions: about 18,000
  plus 40,000 infantry, or about 10,000 plus more than 20,000 cavalry.
- The combined 58,000 infantry figure is therefore a fiscal equivalent, not an
  observed troop reduction and not a claim that those soldiers disappeared in
  439.
- The 461 and 468 values count two major failed attempts to recover Africa. The
  source record does not support one secure combined expedition cost.

## Sources

Peter Heather, *The Fall of the Roman Empire* (2005), supplies the African
fiscal model and the broader external-pressure interpretation. Guy Halsall,
*The Roman West 200–500* (2012), explicitly describes the vicious cycle in
which territorial loss reduced Rome's ability to restore the situation. James
Lacey, *Rome: Strategy of Empire* (2022), independently characterizes the loss
of Carthage as breaking the western tax spine. Bret Devereaux's synthesis is a
reader-facing interpretive cross-check, not sole authority. Novel XIII remains
the bounded primary fiscal anchor.
