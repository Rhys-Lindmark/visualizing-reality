# Rome insight 03 — fiscal evidence audit

## Working claim

Rome did not finance empire with one uniform tax. Provincial land, person,
produce, and customs levies—sometimes collected locally or by contractors—fed
a central system whose dominant recurring expense was the army. At roughly the
height of the Principate, modern reconstructions put military expenditure near
three quarters of the central imperial budget.

## What can be visualized

- Duncan-Jones's two internally consistent budget scenarios for c. 150 and
  c. 215 CE, reproduced by Mattingly. Each scenario's categories sum to its
  published total. The visualization may compare shares within a snapshot.
- Four locally or institutionally bounded rates: Sicily's grain tithe, the
  Gallic customs district, Augustus's inheritance tax, and the auction tax
  assigned to the military treasury.
- Three defensible nominal legionary base-pay anchors through 197 CE.
- The 170 million-sestertius founding transfer to the military treasury, which
  Augustus reports in *Res Gestae* 17.
- Gaius Gracchus's subsidized grain quantity and price, with the disputed
  recipient unit stated rather than silently resolved.

## What must not be visualized as a continuous series

- There is no surviving annual imperial revenue series.
- Provincial tax systems and rates varied; the cited 10%, 5%, 2.5%, and 1%
  values have different bases and cannot be added into one "Roman tax rate."
- The budget estimates are modern reconstructions, not accounts recovered from
  an imperial ledger.
- Nominal sestertii in 150 and 215 CE are not directly comparable measures of
  real resources because prices, coinage, pay, and army size changed.
- The legionary-pay evidence becomes substantially less secure after 197 CE;
  this dataset stops rather than filling the gap.

## Interpretive chain

Devereaux's public syntheses are useful for structure: Republican provincial
tribute grew out of army requisition; a thin central administration often
devolved assessment to communities or contracted collection; and grain or
money raised in one region could support armies or the capital elsewhere.
Hopkins's taxes-and-trade model makes the larger claim that fiscal transfers
could stimulate interregional trade, but Hopkins explicitly describes the
model and its quantitative foundations as tentative. The live insight should
therefore show the fiscal machine and its expenditure priority, not claim a
precise annual flow across the whole empire.
